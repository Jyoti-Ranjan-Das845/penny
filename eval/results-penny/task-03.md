**CPS evidence on the Children (Abolition of Defence of Reasonable Punishment) (Wales) Bill**

The Crown Prosecution Service gave evidence to the Children, Young People and Education Committee — Barry Hughes (Chief Crown Prosecutor for Wales) doing nearly all the talking, with Kwame Biney (CPS senior policy advisor) and Iwan Jenkins (head of complex casework, CPS Cymru Wales). Hughes backs the Bill, and most of the session was him explaining why he thinks it will change very little in practice and still be worth doing.

**Where the law sits now.** Assault offences cover children and adults alike (Offences Against the Person Act 1861, Children and Young Persons Act 1933), but section 58 of the Children Act 2004 gives anyone accused of common assault on a child the reasonable punishment defence. There's no equivalent defence for assaulting an adult, so on Hughes's reading children have fewer protections than adults — and less protection than they get themselves once they turn 16. Worth flagging: the committee understood that age limit to be 18; Hughes said he was "reasonably confident" it's 16, and the point was left to be checked afterwards.

He didn't accept that the 2007 review settles anything. The law should track what society finds acceptable — his comparison was marital rape, an offence only recently — and research since then shows younger people find smacking less acceptable than older generations do.

**How much clearer would it be.** Clearer, he said, but not much clearer. The 2004 Act already removed the defence for ABH, GBH and child cruelty; leaving common assault inside it was a deliberate choice at the time. He described a spectrum. Grabbing a five-year-old out of the path of traffic is technically an assault and will never see a courtroom. A single light smack on the leg after that is probably reasonable chastisement today, depending on the child's size, the force, whether it's a pattern. Repeated smacking, shouting, a blow to the head — that moves toward the criminal end. Removing the defence doesn't remove the grey in the middle; it moves where the grey gets resolved.

**That shift is the real substance.** The CPS applies a two-stage test: enough evidence for a realistic prospect of conviction, then public interest. Today a light smack rarely reaches the CPS at all, because the police can see the defence applies. Without the defence, more cases clear the evidential stage and the public interest test does the filtering. Hughes expects a one-off light smack with no history would fail that test. But on repeat behaviour it bites much faster — his supermarket example: smacking a four-year-old who keeps pulling cans off the shelf is covered now and would still be covered next week, but a third incident with no defence available would reach the public interest test quickly.

Burden and standard of proof are untouched. If a defendant says the contact was lawful, the Crown still has to disprove it beyond reasonable doubt. He expects some people to refuse an out-of-court disposal and contest on principle, and that's the route to an actual conviction — a criminal record and its effect on someone's prospects, for an act that wouldn't have produced one in England.

**Numbers.** Low single figures of prosecutions a year, possibly none; he doubts police referrals would even be double the number prosecuted. That estimate rests on the justice impact assessment, New Zealand research, CPS data from an FOI response, the CPS papers submitted to the 2007 review, and a conversation with Matt Jukes at South Wales Police. Volumes are too small to justify a flag on the England-and-Wales case management system (surprisingly expensive, he said, so it wouldn't happen), so CPS Wales would keep a manual log instead. After 12 to 24 months he'd want a local scrutiny involvement panel — the format they already use for racially aggravated offending — going through real files with the police, third sector bodies and ideally Welsh Government.

Suzy Davies pushed the obvious question: if almost nobody is prosecuted, why use criminal law at all rather than a civil route to drive the culture change? Hughes's answer was that the chance of protection is low but higher than it is now, and that the value lies in the message the law sends.

**Cross-border.** This is the sharp end. The defence survives in England, so the same smack is defensible on the M4 past Bristol and not after the crossing. He thinks the divergence is manageable: the Scottish drink-drive limit is a bigger trap for the public, and Wales already has offences England doesn't (electronic shock collars on pets, cockle picking in west Wales). The CPS code already provides for Welsh divergence, and guidance and charging standards would be updated — light touch on the evidential stage, more substantial work on public interest. He didn't press for a memorandum of understanding, and said CPS involvement with Welsh Government so far has been enough without being heavy. Operationally he'd train two or three specialists in Wales so every case goes to the same small network. Welsh staff are broadly aware already; English prosecutors need briefing because staff move across the border and prosecute in Welsh courts. Nothing is planned by way of public awareness raising in England, and ignorance of the law is no defence.

On awareness generally: the Scottish Bill puts a duty on government to raise awareness and this one doesn't. He wouldn't say whether it should, only that the more awareness there is, the more likely the Act achieves what it's for — and that the media appetite for the story (coverage reached New York) is worth using.

**Two things he wouldn't or couldn't help with.** He declined to produce draft public interest guidance for the committee to see before it votes — cart before horse, since the work is adding specificity to principles already in the code. Davies said plainly that the public interest test is the critical piece and had wanted to see it before deciding. He also can't speak to stopping cases before arrest, which matters because an arrest shows up on a DBS check even if nothing follows: the CPS has no power to direct the police. On out-of-court disposals, the CPS is working with DCC Sara Glen (NPCC lead) on simplifying the range, but Wales can only build an infrastructure of disposals and hope it gets used — it can't direct the police or the CPS towards it, and he said he wasn't the right person to say whose job building it is.

**Two loose legal ends.** Where the parents are the suspects, the CPS would get the child's view through the police rather than from the parents, which he expects to be genuinely difficult in separation cases where children are being used against each other; the child's view is a factor, not determinative. And if a child is offered the choice between a quick smack and fifteen minutes in their room and picks the smack, nobody knows. Consent is well-mapped for serious assaults (Brown, tattooing, branding, tongue-splitting) and undeveloped below that — in his words, it would need to be developed.

Implementation: roughly two years after Royal Assent, guidance fit for purpose by 2022, minor financial consequences and no impact on CPS performance in the meantime.
