# CPS evidence session: Children (Abolition of Defence of Reasonable Punishment) (Wales) Bill

## Session context

This was the eleventh evidence session on the Children (Abolition of Defence of Reasonable Punishment) (Wales) Bill, held by the Children, Young People and Education Committee under the chairmanship of Lynne Neagle AM. The CPS sent three witnesses: Barry Hughes, Chief Crown Prosecutor for Wales; Kwame Biney, senior policy advisor at CPS national headquarters; and Iwan Jenkins, head of the complex casework unit at CPS Cymru Wales. Hughes led nearly all substantive responses. The Bill proposes to abolish the section 58 Children Act 2004 defence of reasonable punishment, which currently allows adults to rely on that defence when charged with common assault on a child.

## Current law and what abolishing the defence changes

Section 58 of the Children Act 2004 permits the "reasonable punishment" defence only for common assault; it does not extend to actual bodily harm, grievous bodily harm, or child cruelty. Adults have no equivalent defence, so the law currently affords children less protection than it affords adults.

Hughes's view was that removing the defence will make the law clearer—but "clearer" is not the same as "simple." A spectrum runs from contact that is plainly non-criminal (grabbing a child from the path of traffic) through ambiguous single smacks to serious, repeated hitting. Abolition moves the grey zone but does not eliminate it.

The CPS operates a two-stage test drawn from the Code for Crown Prosecutors. Stage one asks whether there is sufficient evidence for a realistic prospect of conviction; stage two asks whether prosecution is in the public interest. Stage two is only reached if stage one is first satisfied. Removing the reasonable punishment defence means more cases will clear the evidential stage—because the defence that previously made conviction unrealistic is gone—but the public interest stage will then filter many of them out. A single light smack with no history of violence or concern would, in Hughes's view, likely fail the public interest test even without the defence.

Hughes also framed the change in terms of legal consistency: the defence creates an asymmetry in that it ceases to be available when a child turns 16—a point Lynne Neagle had suggested was 18, but Hughes corrected to 16, noting he was "reasonably confident" of that age. He described law as something that evolves to reflect contemporary social norms and considered the defence inconsistent with the direction of that travel.

## England–Wales divergence and cross-border implications

Because policing and the CPS are not devolved, the National Assembly cannot impose duties on the CPS without the Secretary of State's consent. The divergence created by the Bill is therefore asymmetric: Wales would abolish the defence while England retains it.

Hughes identified two concrete implications. First, awareness: a person who has just crossed from England into Wales loses a legal defence that was available to them moments earlier on the English side of the M4. Second, precedent: Wales would not be the first jurisdiction to create a cross-border divergence with England. Scotland's lower drink-drive limit—22 µg per 100 ml of breath against 35 µg in England and Wales—already operates in this way, and the CPS has demonstrated it can handle this through the existing provisions of its Code.

On guidance, Hughes expected to produce two kinds: a light-touch evidential-stage note for prosecutors reminding them that the defence is unavailable in Wales, and more detailed public-interest guidance. Both are achievable within the projected two-year implementation window, and no memorandum of understanding between Welsh and English authorities would be needed. A small cadre of two or three CPS specialists in Wales would handle all referrals under the Bill; prosecutors who work across the England–Wales border would need to be briefed, and CPS England would need some awareness, though not at the same depth.

The committee asked whether the CPS could share draft public-interest guidance with scrutinisers before they voted. Hughes advised against it: the guidance would adapt existing public interest factors with Bill-specific detail and needs to develop alongside practice. He described providing draft guidance at this stage as "putting the cart before the horse."

On awareness more broadly, Hughes noted that ignorance of the law provides no defence, making public awareness-raising important. He also noted the Bill had already attracted international media coverage and recommended the Welsh Government leverage that interest.

## Implementation: readiness, data, and costs

The CPS welcomed the anticipated transition period of roughly two years between Royal Assent and the legislation taking effect, as Hughes confirmed in response to a question from Suzy Davies: the CPS expects to be "fit for purpose" by 2022.

On the burden of proof, Hughes was explicit: nothing changes. The prosecution must still prove beyond reasonable doubt that an unlawful assault occurred. If a defendant argues the act was lawful, the Crown must disprove that to the criminal standard; the defence is not assumed, and its removal does not shift the burden.

Pre-arrest decisions—including whether to arrest and whether to caution—lie entirely with the police. The CPS cannot direct the police at that stage. Hughes acknowledged that a DBS (Disclosure and Barring Service) record can follow an arrest even where no charge results, but characterised this as a police decision rather than a CPS one.

On data collection, a system-wide flag across England and Wales to capture Bill-related cases would be technically expensive and impractical. The CPS would instead keep a manual log of referrals and prosecutions under the Bill—manageable because expected case volumes are very low.

Financial implications are minor for the pre-enactment period, and no significant financial consequences are anticipated overall, consistent with the low volume of cases expected. The existing coordination network—a strategic implementation group and the liaison between Hughes's office and Kwame Biney's national headquarters team—was described by Hughes as "perfectly sufficient" to prepare guidance that works across both jurisdictions.

## Prosecutions, the public interest test, and anticipated volumes

Hughes explained how the public interest test (stage two of the Code) would work in practice. It is calibrated to proportionality and takes into account the age of the offender and victim, the circumstances, the impact on the victim, the impact on the suspect, and any prior history. More serious, repeated, or historical offending increases the likelihood of prosecution; a single incident with no history does not automatically result in a prosecution decision.

Where parents are both suspects, the CPS would seek the child's view through the police rather than through the parents. The child's perspective is a factor in the public interest assessment, not determinative. Hughes noted a specific difficulty: children in acrimonious separation or custody disputes can be used instrumentally, and the CPS already has well-developed tools for assessing evidence from parties with a stake in the outcome.

A question about child consent to assault arose. Hughes noted that consent is available as a defence in some contexts—tattooing, for example—but not others, citing *R v Brown* as the authority excluding it for serious sadomasochistic acts. For lesser assaults, "the law is more unclear," he said, and would need to be developed through case law and practice.

On anticipated volumes, Hughes expected referrals and prosecutions to be very low. A 2007 Government review found 12 cases across approximately two years for all of England and Wales. A more recent FOI request produced a figure of roughly three cases per year nationally. Hughes described this second figure as "not entirely reliable, but indicative." Wales accounts for roughly 5% of national prosecutions, suggesting prosecutions under the Bill might run to low single figures annually—possibly zero in some years.

Hughes addressed directly the argument that low prosecution numbers make the legislation pointless. There are approximately 10,000 criminal offences on the statute book; only around 5% are prosecuted in any given year. The law's function, he argued, is to signal what society considers acceptable behaviour, and prosecution numbers are not its primary measure of effect.

## Out-of-court disposals

Primary responsibility for out-of-court disposals lies with the police, not the CPS. At the national level, the National Police Chiefs' Council—led on this by Deputy Chief Constable Sara Glen—is working on simplifying the existing range of disposals. The CPS can choose an out-of-court disposal even after the police have made a referral for prosecution, if the CPS takes a different view of the public interest; but once a disposal is issued, the CPS neither administers nor monitors compliance with its conditions—that is the police's role.

The committee explored whether Wales could build a Bill-specific disposal infrastructure. Hughes's view was that it is possible, but it would have to be constructed by the Welsh Government, local authorities, the police, or police and crime commissioners using their commissioning powers. Neither the CPS nor the police can be directed toward such an infrastructure, but a well-built one would attract use in practice.

Scotland's legislation includes a government duty to promote awareness; the Welsh Bill contains no equivalent. Hughes declined to express a view on whether such a duty would be appropriate, but stated clearly that the greater the awareness of the changed law, the more likely the Act would achieve its purpose.

## Unintended consequences

**Criminal records.** Someone convicted in Wales for conduct that would not have led to a conviction in England would carry a criminal record that their English equivalent would not. Hughes described such cases as isolated and noted they would generally involve an element of choice on the defendant's part—specifically, declining an out-of-court disposal and insisting on being prosecuted.

**Performance targets.** The CPS has no conviction targets. National police sanction-detection targets were abolished years ago, though some individual constabularies may retain internal targets. Hughes considered inappropriate prosecutions driven by performance metrics to be "extremely unlikely," and noted that the volumes involved would be too small to affect any metric meaningfully.

**Malicious reporting.** The potential for increased malicious reporting in acrimonious separation or custody situations was acknowledged, but Hughes was not concerned. The CPS already has well-developed tools for evaluating evidence from parties with a direct stake in the outcome.

**ECHR.** The 2007 Government position was that section 58 as drafted is ECHR-compliant. Hughes observed that if Wales enacts the Bill, Wales would become more compliant with the Convention while England would arguably become less so. The position is likely to be tested through case law rather than an immediate direct challenge. He declined to speculate on the future direction of European jurisprudence.

**Registered intermediaries for child witnesses.** A shortage of registered intermediaries in Wales existed at one point. The Ministry of Justice took action, and numbers have since increased. Hughes considered the shortage a potential barrier rather than a real one given the expected very low volume of cases under the Bill, and noted that demand would drive further recruitment if needed.
