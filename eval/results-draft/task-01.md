# Product Design Meeting Summary

## Meeting Context

This was the team's first kick-off meeting, chaired by Sarah, the Project Manager. Four roles were present: Project Manager, User Interface, Industrial Designer, and Marketing. Sarah set the agenda for the 25-minute session: introductions, tool training, discussion of the project plan, and sharing initial ideas. The meeting opened with an icebreaker in which each participant drew their favourite animal on the whiteboard and described its characteristics.

## Project Brief and Design Phases

The team is designing a remote control. Sarah described the product goals as stated in the brief: it should be original (not already on the market), trendy, appealing to a wide market, and user-friendly across all ages — she used the phrase "grannies to kids."

Sarah outlined three sequential design phases:

1. **Functional design** — establishing what needs the product must fulfil, what effects it must have, and how it will achieve them.
2. **Conceptual design** — determining the overall direction and thinking.
3. **Detailed design** — working out how to put the concept into practice.

## Financial Parameters and Market Scope

Sarah shared the following figures from the project brief:

- **Selling price:** 25 Euros (the team estimated roughly 17 Pounds at the exchange rate discussed, which they treated as approximate).
- **Production cost:** 12.50 Euros — about half the selling price, in Sarah's words.
- **Profit aim:** 50 million Euros. When Marketing asked whether this was a first-year target, Sarah confirmed it but qualified it: "I presume so."
- **Market:** international (Sarah cited Australia and America as examples); all age groups; not focused on any specific business segment.

The Industrial Designer noted that comparable products currently on the market cost around 20 Pounds, which gave the team a reference point for competitiveness. Marketing raised the question of whether to price below that figure or risk the product appearing cheap — this was left unresolved.

## Design Discussion: Ideas and Constraints

The team moved from financial parameters into a broader conversation about design, grounded in their own experiences with remote controls.

**Shared pain points** that framed the discussion:
- Universal remotes that lose their programmed signals (raised by the Project Manager from personal experience).
- Too many small buttons with small text — Sarah described hating having to search through "a million tiny little buttons with tiny little words."
- Symbols that are unclear or that users feel they should understand but do not (raised by Marketing).

**Ideas proposed:**

1. **LCD display with menu navigation** (Marketing, then User Interface independently converging): Marketing suggested incorporating a mobile-phone-style menu display with a small LCD screen to reduce button count. User Interface had been thinking along the same lines, adding that familiarity with mobile phone interfaces might make navigation easier for users.

2. **Intuitive icons and self-explaining design** (Industrial Designer): Drawing on newer phones that use pictures rather than text labels in menus, the Industrial Designer suggested that a picture-based menu system could make the device more self-explanatory across age groups.

3. **Dual-function layout** (Project Manager): Sarah proposed keeping the core buttons — volume, channels, play/pause — visible at the top level, with a separate menu section for advanced functions such as recording and options. The goal was that picking up the device should not make it look complicated.

4. **Flip-top form factor** (Marketing): A flip-top design was suggested so that opening the device reveals a larger screen, keeping the device compact when closed. User Interface noted this might save space and avoid a bulky appearance, but flagged possible cost implications; the Project Manager also raised cost as a concern.

5. **Large-print / accessibility mode** (Marketing): An extra button to increase text size on screen, analogous to large-print books, was proposed for older users. Marketing acknowledged that this would display less content simultaneously but argued readability was the priority.

6. **Screen cost constraint** (Industrial Designer): At a production cost of 12.50 Euros, the Industrial Designer considered a high-quality display unrealistic, suggesting a black-and-white LCD screen would be the more likely outcome.

**Fashion and materials:** The Industrial Designer mentioned having looked at the company website, where the brand is described as "we put the fashion into electronics" — presenting a fashionable look as a design expectation rather than an optional enhancement. Marketing's view was that the device need not be metal; lightweight plastic could still be styled attractively and is easier to mould. Sarah cited Telewest's silver plastic remote as an example of plastic looking smart. No final decisions were reached on form factor, screen type, or materials; the discussion was framed as direction-setting for individual work.

**Older-user accessibility:** The team acknowledged that an LCD menu interface may not fully solve the accessibility challenge for older users. Sarah's example was a grandparent who could answer a mobile phone but would not attempt texting; the Industrial Designer countered that their own grandparent used teletext more capably than they did, illustrating that older-user ability varies. The team treated this as an ongoing design constraint rather than a resolved question.

## Next Steps

Sarah closed the meeting by giving the team 30 minutes for individual work. The group planned to reconvene afterwards, at which point Sarah would give another presentation. No specific deliverables or task assignments per person were named during the meeting.
