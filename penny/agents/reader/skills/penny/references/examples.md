# Illustrations, not templates

These neutral, fictional examples illustrate decisions: what to keep, repair,
incorporate, or leave out. They are not the full range of good writing. Do not
copy their sentence patterns, informality, or structure into unrelated work.
No message needs a fragment, a smiley, an admission, or a particular opening.
If a draft resembles an example, check whether that resemblance serves the
author and reader or merely echoes the example. Facts do not transfer either.

## A light proofread

Source:

> A preview of the numbers we have confidence on. Better than I expected. :-)
> Still checking the cost difference, will update when that's done.

Light edit:

> A preview of the numbers we have confidence in. Better than I expected. :-)
> Still checking the cost difference. Will update when that's done.

Repair the distracting phrasing and punctuation. The fragment, reaction, and
smiley belong to this source and relationship; they are not a voice formula.

## Plain sentences are human too

Facts: the requested file is attached; one estimate remains provisional and
will be replaced after a review. No completion date is supplied.

> The file is attached. The final estimate is still provisional; we’ll update
> it after the review.

No extra warmth, deadline, or framing is needed. If the user asks only for
grammar, do not invent a warmer relationship or a more elaborate explanation.

## Incorporate a lesson when that is the purpose

Request: update an existing launch checklist using a test report where useful.
The report says an interrupted transfer lost progress and repeating the transfer
recovered it. It also describes the room where the test took place.

An applicable checklist item:

> Check that an interrupted transfer can resume without losing progress.

The room is irrelevant. A section retelling the test is not needed unless the
checklist also needs provenance. Do not assert recovery is already implemented.
If the request instead asks for a test summary, report the observation and its
limits. The same source has a different role in the two artifacts.

## Requirements are not assignments

Request: describe what is needed for a trial, without allocating work. Source:
test samples and a way to record results are needed; no allocation is agreed.

> The trial needs representative samples and a record of the results.

“The coordinator will supply the samples by Friday” is still an invented
assignment even though it contains no person's name. In contrast, for an
agreed-action summary, preserve “Jo will send the samples on Friday” when the
user supplies that commitment. Do not turn a local correction into a ban on
names or dates.

## Keep distinct findings distinct

Facts: one test failed because an input was missing; another requires an
external reference to determine the expected result.

“Both tests show the expected answers are wrong” is not supported. Neither is
“Two findings point the same way” useful without saying how. State each finding,
or state a supported implication: both inputs and reference requirements need
checking before attributing the failures to the processor.
